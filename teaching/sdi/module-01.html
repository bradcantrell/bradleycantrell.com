Week 01 — Exercise 1.3

The first week focuses on case studies — architecture and landscape architecture projects in varied scales, locations, and contexts — and learning how to read, re-draw and re-interpret the case studies through hand-drawn diagrams and notational exercises. Case studies that share similar formal, material, programmatic or contextual conditions are used to connect the disciplines. Understanding the importance of drawing — not only as a tool to represent architecture and landscape but also as a tool to design — is key during this week.

Objectives

  • Understand drawing as a new form of language and expression.
  • Learn to approach design issues from multiple angles, perspectives and scales.
  • Be able to diagram and represent complex ideas.
  • Extract lessons on design, scale, proportion, atmosphere, etc.
  • Express rigorously ideas and architectural forms.
  • Understand the hierarchy of the different graphic elements that we have available.
  • Understand and transmit relationships between elements of a set.

Exercises

Exercise 1.1 — Extract: Research

Find, review, and read additional information on your assigned case study and its architect(s) and landscape architect(s). Cross-reference between plans, sections, images and descriptive text until you understand the different spaces that compose the project, and how one might have been intended to move through and inhabit these spaces. Look closely at the materials and how they relate to one another; become familiar with the geographical, climatic and historical context; and learn about the characteristics of the designers' processes of drawing and making.

Deliverables: pin up all information found on your case study; print-out of key words/phrases from readings; sketches in your sketchbook showing process; working bibliography in APA style.

Due: Tuesday, July 16, morning.

Exercise 1.2 — Abstract: Analyze, Diagram & Sketch

Draw one feature of the project at a time, striving to extract what is most important. Generate a series of diagrams to understand and explain the key relationships embedded in your case study — a diagram is an extraction and an abstraction rather than comprehensive illustration. Pare down your graphic communication to lineweight and linetype; drawings to be carefully hand drawn, grayscale, to scale, on trace paper trimmed to sheets of the same size.

[A] Descriptive Diagrams — pulling the project apart piece by piece, covering nine categories: spatial organization, circulation, structure, program, light, intimate vs. collective space, materials, terrain, and a wild card idiosyncratic to your project.

[B] Synthesis Diagrams — overlaying analytical diagrams two or three at a time to reveal new insights and draw conclusions about how the architecture works spatially, experientially and materially.

Deliverables: minimum 12 draft descriptive diagrams on trace paper over scaled plan or section (covering all 9 categories); minimum one draft synthesis diagram combining multiple descriptive diagrams.

Due: Thursday, July 18, morning.

Exercise 1.3 — Depict: Precise & Considered Representation

Redraw the case study with the aid of CAD software, becoming familiar with the prevailing way of drawing in the world of design. Emphasis is placed on the duality of the plan and section as basic components of representation — linetype hierarchies, lineweights, depths, and the choice of what is drawn.

Deliverables: minimum one plan of all levels present in the project; minimum four sections; a tidy CAD file with layers and layouts; a parti diagram of your case study at a designated scale.

Due: Friday, July 19, morning.

Workshops

  • Workshop 001 — Analog Drawing. Hand drawing as a new tool of expression — diagrams, sketches, process information, rigorous plans and sections.
  • Workshop 002 — CAD Drawing. AutoCAD basics, emphasizing rigor and hierarchy to achieve precision and expressive quality.
  • Workshop 003 — Printing and Scale. Scale and layout — presenting work in a rigorous yet appealing way.

Readings

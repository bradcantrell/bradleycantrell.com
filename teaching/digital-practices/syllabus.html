Introduction

Meeting Times: Tuesday, 12:30–13:30.

Faculty: Bradley Cantrell, Chair and Professor of Landscape Architecture.

Office Hours: Tuesday, 13:30–15:30, Campbell Hall 230 · bcantrell@virginia.edu.

Representing landscape phenomena is fundamentally temporal, grounded in the notion that environments are continually in flux. Temporality is a given — leaves rustle in the wind, soil compacts under our feet, water slides across surfaces and permeates into loose substrates. People gather and disperse, the market rises, housing prices fall, and the seasons march along with the death of Winter into the life of the Spring, each signaling the march of time as day to night.

Digital Practices IV focuses on the representation of landscape phenomena using the diorama as a conceptual framework. The diorama provides a grounded space to explore landscape phenomena through the construction of the orthographic projection. The diorama refers to a three-dimensional representation of a scene or landscape, typically viewed from a controlled vantage point. Using a static viewpoint each student will represent an animated view highlighting the ephemeral and temporal qualities of the landscape.

The Project

The concept of the diorama will be explored through a single 90″–150″ long animation that will be developed throughout the term. The animation will be from a selected viewpoint; the orthographic plan or section, the axonometric, or the perspectival. The viewpoint should not change significantly throughout the animation, creating a limited view of the scene. The focus of the animation should be on the elements, atmospheres, and effects that bring the landscape view to life. In conjunction with the animated landscape each project will also explore how to incorporate forms of annotation and notation to highlight real and perceived performance data, diagrammatic relationships, and didactic overlays. It is important that the correct balance of ambiguous experience and discreet diagramming are found to produce a compelling and engaging animation.

The media and tools used to create the animation are determined by each student. A series of After Effects workshops will be conducted to introduce basic to advanced motion graphics concepts. The animation can be created directly in After Effects or may explore a hybrid solution that incorporates digital (3D animation, Rhino, 3ds Max, Maya) or physical models (stop-motion/time lapse). The project will be reviewed throughout the semester with specific benchmarks and deliverables outlined in the course schedule. Each animation will be guided and grounded in a narrative concept and series of technique and aesthetic precedents. These may come from a broad range of interests; film, historic techniques, popular culture, etc. The cases will be reviewed throughout the semester and the final iteration should serve as an accompaniment to the animation/film as a series of cards or a small booklet that is designed within the overall aesthetic/narrative. It is feasible to focus on concept, precedent, and/or technique depending on interest.

Course Structure

Lectures (4 weeks) highlight a range of topics that situate animation, annotation, notation, and the diorama in historic and contemporary media techniques and concepts.

Workshops (4 weeks) cover basic motion graphics topics in Adobe After Effects. Beyond the workshops students should explore their own techniques and methods (be curious).

Project and Reviews (4 reviews) — a personal animation project derived from a proposed narrative and the aesthetic and technique precedents.

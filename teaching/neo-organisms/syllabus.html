Introduction

Meeting Times: Monday 12:00–17:30 and Thursday 09:00–13:00.

Teaching Faculty: Bradley Cantrell, Chair and Professor (Campbell Hall 230, bcantrell@virginia.edu). Teaching Fellow: Zihao Zhang (zz3ub@virginia.edu).

The interface between anthropogenic and evolved systems requires dissolving boundaries and erasing hierarchies within human and non-human agents. The studio will explore these boundaries and hierarchies through the physical and cultural definitions of wilderness in the context of new technologies that enable local and remote sensing, long-term monitoring, and machine intelligence. Students will be asked to engage their research agenda through the concept of neo-organisms and wetware (protocols) to speculate on the implementation of adaptive landscapes that are curated and choreographed for a broad range of species and processes.

The format for the course is a research studio in which each student will be required to formulate their unique research agenda. Workshops, readings, and discussions will provide a broad framework to experiment with concepts in responsive technologies, robotics, machine intelligence, and animation/simulation to imagine new forms of infrastructure and wildness.

The Mississippi River Delta

The external image of Louisiana is a fantasy, a collective imagination of Mardi Gras beads, alligators, cypress trees, and crawfish. Much like the choreographed view that portrays a prosperous regime and satisfied populace in North Korea, traveling to Louisiana as an outsider presents a façade of natural ecosystems and abundance. Beneath this façade is a rotting corpse, slowly eroding, and sinking into the Gulf of Mexico due to exploitation, human settlement, and blunt engineering. Within this territory exists a wildness; remote areas that are difficult to access, species that are threatening to humans, extreme sites of extraction, and powerful hydrologic infrastructures. How do we engage this wildness? Is it possible to reimagine and regenerate the delta without abstracting and generalizing, homogenizing and simplifying? In a highly manipulated, managed, and exploited landscape is it possible to also have wildness, the unknown, the powerful and uncontrolled while also promoting humanity?

The condition of the Mississippi River Delta is dire and our work as designers is not to find a solution that “restores” or “saves” this marginalized environment. The studio will engage the delta as a test bed that is examined, poked, and prodded through remote and mediated methods of observation and modification. The remote view of the delta will be assumed as the norm; the site as experienced through human senses will be only one factor that is put alongside an array of other modes of mediated experience, data collection, and monitoring.

Thinking of the delta itself as the model, the landscape as the media itself, the studio will take a critical look at the long history of modeling and simulation that has formed and reformed this territory. It will then be necessary to develop protocols to choreograph processes and modes of feedback rather than assume the methods of modeling and representation need ever greater precision. The act of modifying (no matter how small) is just as important to highly precise models when trying to engage the environment — the act of modification is scalar: temporally, physically, and conceptually.

Lastly the research will engage the technologies that have created the delta in its current form — the river infrastructure, the monitoring systems, and the logistics infrastructures — to imagine how new technologies (machine learning, artificial intelligence, simulation, and robotics) can play a future role in the reformulation of a managed delta. What do these technologies afford and how can a broader range of species, phenomena, and cultures be catalyzed within the territory?

Method

The site for the studio will be southern Louisiana broadly, focusing primarily on the interface between settlement (habitat) and associated landscape systems. The first weeks of the studio will ask students to define the bounds of their investigation and to situate themselves within this dynamic and fluctuating deltaic landscape. Using a fuzzy definition of site, the studio will use the concept of the microcosm as a way to formulate a representation or simulation — the basis for all investigations.

The first phase involves site research and the construction of an onto-cartography that maps site phenomena, processes, and interactions. This onto-cartography will function as the relational diagram that will guide the micro-cosm (testing grounds) you develop next. The term onto-cartography comes from Levi Bryant’s assertion that everything can be construed as a machine with specific interactions with other machines. Machines in this sense are multi-layered, simple/complex, and open/closed — they represent not only physical elements but also bodies of knowledge, ideologies, politics, and/or economies. The onto-cartographic framework is rooted in object oriented ontology and presumes that the object (machines) and its relationships with other objects (machines) constitute the whole.

In conjunction with the onto-cartographic site research, a micro-cosm will be developed to test, prototype, and generate protocols (wetware) for future neo-organisms (infrastructures). Conceptually, the micro-cosm is a self-contained environment with limited connections to the external environment. The introduction of new elements, or modifications of the system, can then be monitored and observed to understand its effects. The micro-cosm will create a methodology that engages media as a performative act — a methodology to develop protocols for interaction and to find moments of chance and reveal the unexpected through rigorous and well documented experiments.

Each student will form a manifesto in conjunction with the onto-cartography and micro-cosm that outlines their intent through an articulated stance. The manifesto should adopt the premise of the studio and form a critical stance that promotes or rejects the overarching framework. The studio is a platform to test, experiment, and possibly fail — each student should bring their own agenda and augment and expand the resources presented in studio.

All work in the studio must be developed through continual feedback between your colleagues and the instructor. Projects should develop iteratively throughout the term; the work presented at the reviews is the product of the discussions and critiques at desk-crits and pin-ups. Due to the open learning environment it is necessary that each student fully engage in the pin-ups, providing feedback and producing work to review and discuss. Attendance at all studio meetings, pin-ups, and reviews is mandatory.

Introduction

Meeting Times: Wednesday 9:00–11:30

Teaching Faculty: Bradley Cantrell, Professor and Chair. Teaching Assistant (2019): Thatch Gerike, MLA 2020.

The current condition of the anthropocene is one of ecological hybridity, an evolved state where constructed megaliths exist within noisy gradients of the pseudo natural. These landscapes, the hybridized territories, are the product of the exertion of civil engineering, environmental management, and the pressures of urbanization. Where does the discipline of landscape architecture find its footing in the ecologically complex and technologically abstract realm of contemporary society? To confront the intractable issues of the anthropocene it is necessary to unpack the technologies that operate at the scale of the territory. Therefore, the interface between the built environment and evolved/biological/geological systems requires a blurring of strategies that address nascent forms of simulation, sensing, logistics, biological engineering, and technological interface to question the dichotomy of the constructed and the natural.

To promote a projective language for landscape architecture, it is important to understand the influence that infrastructures impress upon landscape systems. Looking back to understand how “contemporary” technologies were deployed to construct current infrastructures, the course will present a methodology for landscape architecture to engage in territorial scale technologies. The framework for this approach will be to first research and represent the construction and influence of built infrastructures; second, to form a general understanding of modeling and simulation tools to be able to deploy design simulation tools; and third, to unpack the practice of surveying a site and to construct sensing instruments.

In this course we will take the stance that to engage the territory requires the application of reflexive and responsive technologies, to form new relationships through simulation, sensing, and feedback, and to embrace novel ecological systems. These novel forms of ecology demand a deconstruction of current forms of settlement, infrastructure, and biological systems to understand the constructed landscape as a synthesizer of biotic and abiotic processes. The interstices of these new relationships become the medium in which the course examines new potentials for construction, sensing, monitoring, and automation as a starting point to propose new hydrological, productive, and logistic infrastructures. Cyborg Ecologies posits that the role of landscape architecture as implementers of new technologies is to promote active and real-time methods of environmental stewardship and management. These methodologies span a range of scales, from micro adjustments of chemical processes to automated construction to regional management and monitoring to global remote sensing systems. Primarily, the concept of cyborg ecologies creates an applied, recursive, or iterative relationship between computation and biology (amongst other landscape processes).

The course offers an overview of landscape infrastructural systems through lectures, workshops, and student research at various scales and promotes a projective view of the implementation of and interaction with nascent technologies for simulation, monitoring, and automation.

The Three Exercises

Exercise 01: Deconstructing Infrastructure

Exercise 01 asks students to research an assigned infrastructure and represent its construction, management, and influence within the landscape.

Exercise 02: Simulating Phenomena

Exercise 02 uses computer simulations to understand the basic numerical models that allow for the computation of water movement, atmospherics, and thermodynamics. Using these tools each student is asked to formulate how these tools may be deployed, represented, hacked, and/or misused to satisfy a projective agenda for design generation.

Project 03: Sensing the Environment

Exercise 03 examines contemporary methods of surveying, sensing, and monitoring in the environment. Workshops introduce DIY physical computing using Arduino and connections to tools in Grasshopper using the plugin Firefly. Teams of students create a sensing and monitoring methodology (software/hardware) that addresses local and/or remote sources.

Course Objectives

  1. Understand the construction and influence of mega-infrastructure within the territory.
  2. Identify simulation tools and develop an understanding for the underlying numerical models.
  3. Create sensing devices and visualizations that probe the local to the remote.

Course Readings

The course readings overlap with LAR 7020 Foundation Studio IV. Readings are assigned with each exercise throughout the semester, and there are three reading discussions across the semester. Students present (3 slides) and discuss readings from the assigned reading list, signing up for one of the weeks.

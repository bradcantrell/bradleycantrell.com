Overview

Term: Fall 2018 · 3 Semester Hours. Meets: Monday 10:00–11:00 am (CAM 302A) and Thursday 5:30–7:00 pm (CAM 105).

Instructors: Bradley Cantrell, Chair and Professor, Landscape Architecture (bcantrell@virginia.edu, 230 CAM / Wed 10:00–11:00 am); Bradford Goetz, Assistant Professor, Landscape Architecture (mbg6v@virginia.edu, 409 CAM / Tue 10:00–12:00 pm).

Teaching Assistants: Weaam Alabdullah (PhD), Yu Cheng (MLA 2020), Wanyi Li (MLA 2020), Cong Nie (MLA 2019).

This course introduces new conceptual frameworks on computational thinking, spatial structures, and associative modeling in conjunction with long-standing architectural techniques, technologies, and intra/inter software workflows fundamental to the design process. Terrain, vegetation, circulation, atmosphere, and water organize the semester, situate student learning activities, sequence learning assessments, and support critical, creative, and practical application of skills.

The goal of the course is to introduce students to the fundamental ideas of computational design, as a supplement to 3D modeling and a foundation for later work with topics such as advanced digital fabrication and computational analysis within our program.

Design Computation I is the first of a three-course series that asks students to explore ways of analyzing and representing landscapes through a variety of 2-D (drafting), 3-D (modeling), and 4-D (simulation) models and methods that address design process and production and expose students to the resources, workflows, and concepts utilized in the praxis of landscape architecture.

Objectives

  1. To develop skills for visual thinking via digital techniques, and demonstrate proficiency with digital applications and work-flows including Adobe Creative Suite, AutoCAD, Rhinoceros3D and its parametric modeling/visual programming plug-in Grasshopper.
  2. To introduce a process of designing based on algorithmic thinking.
  3. To fabricate models and develop conceptually-precise annotated operational diagrams that communicate topological and behavioral systems.
  4. To create composite analysis drawings that utilize workflows between AutoCAD, Rhinoceros, Illustrator, and Photoshop.
  5. To use a range of 3d modeling software and parametric tools at a basic level to produce a series of fabricated models.

Structure

Weekly lectures and lab exercises provide the foundation for the group’s collective exploration, research, and discussion. Through a series of working labs, students are exposed to an expanded set of digital techniques for analyzing and generating landform and site processes as a means of advancing both technical and conceptual ability. This format aims to establish fluency in conceptual, organizational, and formal expression as well as to provide a point of departure for an in-depth awareness of landscape precedents and representational techniques.

The course examines a range of digital tools and their historical context through 12 lectures and 10 lab exercises, geared toward developing an understanding of the interrelationships between landform, process, and time, from the geological scale to the human scale. Each Monday, topical lectures present iconographic precedent drawings and time-based media within the landscape architecture canon as well as from affiliated fields, with examples ranging from scientific drawings to contemporary practice. Mondays also introduce new methodologies and tie together representational precedent and theory with technique.

Thursday meetings are lab sessions focused on specific representational techniques with an emphasis on the development of fluid workflows between modeling in Rhino and Grasshopper and graphics and layout in the Adobe Suite. The labs are not meant to be prescriptive — rather, each week teaching assistants present methods, which students are expected to expand upon. Each lab session has a deliverable that is used to further articulate the student’s site.

Assignments

Students complete ten weekly exercises that work cumulatively to explore the complexities of landscape at multiple scales and time periods through both abstract and concrete representational techniques, 2D and 3D projection, and static and dynamic media. The assignments are located within a landscape matrix — a range of landscape typologies that must be mediated in an attempt to represent hybrids and transitions between each region. Each student is assigned a quadrant within this matrix that they develop based on the fixed landscape typologies and in consultation with adjacent sites.

Grades reflect professional standards of ethics, timeliness, and group cooperation, while also allowing room for intellectual and material experimentation. Final grades are a reflection of growth made throughout the semester.

Evaluation

Evaluation places significant emphasis on craft, rigor, legibility, and synthesis of course content:

  • Craft — skill in planning, making, or executing an activity involving making something in a skillful way, whether by hand or digitally: the level of line-work or quality of line, the precision used when digitally cutting out images for a montage, a hierarchy developed for labeling, matching fonts, citing charts, remembering units. {Antonyms: sloppy, careless, mediocre}
  • Rigor — the quality or state of being very exact, careful, or strict: the diligence and resourcefulness needed to investigate a subject beyond a brief Internet search, to research its system of knowledge, to use the library; the rigorous logic needed to create a diagram of a complex issue or process. {Antonyms: lazy, indifferent, imprecise}
  • Legibility — capable of being read or deciphered; discovered or understood: a complex diagram does not need to be complicated — the concentration, trajectory, location, and magnitude of its lines should be purposeful and communicate an idea. {Antonyms: confusing, complicated, messy}
  • Synthesis — something made by combining different things or parts; the combination of often diverse conceptions into a coherent whole; deductive reasoning, or a conclusion reached by logical deduction: discovering and visualizing relationships. {Antonyms: to misunderstand, clumsy, inept}

The “typical” grade for a course should be a B. Exceptional work can be recognized with an A or A-, but this work must exceed the expectation of the course and the assignments — and not merely meet those expectations in interesting ways. Grades less than B- are considered failing grades in this graduate program. Full letter-grade descriptions appear in the complete syllabus PDF.

Software & Web Resources

The course workflow centers on Rhino 3D and the Grasshopper visual programming plugin, with AutoCAD and the Adobe Creative Suite (Illustrator, Photoshop, InDesign) for graphics and layout. Curated online resources include the Architectural Technology Laboratorial Venture (atlv.org), the Generative Design collection at Columbia GSAPP, the Generative Landscapes index of examples, Grasshopper tutorials and discussion forums (grasshopper3d.com, discourse.mcneel.com), the Plethora Project tutorial library, and Lynda.com training (accessed with a UVA NetBadge). The complete annotated resource list appears in the full syllabus PDF.

Honor System & Policy

All graded assignments must be pledged, and collaboration between classmates and between quadrants of the landscape matrix is expected. Always cite any resources or individuals consulted to complete an assignment.

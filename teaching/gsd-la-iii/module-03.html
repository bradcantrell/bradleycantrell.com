Workshop 3 · Buildings + Public Spaces

Prototyping City Fabrics

density · solar exposure · built and open space
Launch: Oct 13Review: Oct 31COV + FAR · Ladybug · shadow studies

Download the full Workshop 3 brief (PDF)

The third and last workshop focuses on the relationship between buildings and open spaces as a driver for infrastructural, social, and ecological opportunities. The mutual influence between buildings and open spaces is explored through the use of density and sun exposure as the main guiding design parameters.

City fabrics are primarily defined through the organization of buildings and open spaces. While many circumstances can regulate the grain of an urban fabric, students are asked to use density and solar exposure as the two main variables regulating the mutual influence of built and open space. Through the variables COV (coverage) and FAR (floor area ratio), density establishes the surface area of open space as well as the volume of built space. The relationship between solar orientation and urban form is of perennial importance to the design of city fabrics — access to the sun is considered an ancient and inviolable right in many cultures, an issue of social equity. Students are accordingly asked to regulate the tensions that exist between built form (through capital accumulation) and access to sunlight (through environmental consensus).

Objectives

  1. To understand that density is defined through the interrelation of two parameters: Coverage (COV) and Floor Area Ratio (FAR).
  2. To work iteratively through volumetric studies that consider sun exposure as a design principle in urban design.
  3. Develop endemic and/or novel organizational relationships between buildings and landscapes (built form and landforms) as the basis for city making.
  4. Explore an approach to the design of urban open space that considers building volumes, street widths, topographic changes, and landform types.

Workshop Method

The workshop follows a design method: (A) select residential types; (B) explore different densities; (C) design the relationship between landforms and built forms according to water flows (stormwater, flooding, etc.); (D) investigate building assemblies and organizations according to solar exposure. Residential types are selected in relationship to the programs considered in Workshop 2, accounting for the temporality of programs (residential floors redeveloped into office space based on changing real estate pressures, building life expectancy, economic cycles, mortgage types). Height restrictions due to aviation approaches to Boston Logan International Airport apply.

Students investigate different building densities according to the landscape conditions found in their projects, producing iterations that explore different combinations of COV and FAR — keeping FAR constant while varying COV, and vice versa. The objective is not the optimization of built form, but the interrelationship between building density and open space. For reference, the brief gives four Boston-area density parameters: Watertown (COV=11%, FAR=0.28), Cambridge (COV=42%, FAR=1.5), Back Bay (COV=65%, FAR=3.8), and the Seaport District (COV=86%, FAR=13).

Four milled models (7.5″ × 7.5″ × 3″) explore a fixed parameter — a fixed COV with a variable FAR, or a fixed FAR with a variable COV. Different building densities are distributed throughout the site with landscape connections designed between them, producing shadow plans (rendering engines or Ladybug) that include buildings, shadows, and contour lines for different times of year. Students then explore different types of urban fabric manipulations to regulate solar exposure — street realignments, reorganizations of the proportions between buildings and open space, and axonometric iterations where built form is sculpted — and explore ways in which buildings can engage the ground according to residential and landform types (sitting on flat ground, embedded on sloped ground, hovering over smaller landforms).

Workshop Site

The site is a portion of the larger South Boston studio site. The site is in an urban environment, surrounded by adjacent neighborhoods and multi-use districts on all sides except the edge that engages the water, which must be accounted for in residential types, building heights, and the size of open spaces. Boston has four distinct seasons, and solar exposure should account for seasonal shadow differences; the brief gives the location 42.34, −71.03 (42°20′24″N, 71°01′48″W) for determining solar paths and sun angles.

Deliverables

  • Genealogical tree(s) — 24″ × 36″ digital drawing, interrelating all iterations generated throughout the three workshops.
  • Residential types — 18″ × 24″ digital drawing, updating the programmatic taxonomy from Workshop 2.
  • Density studies — sixteen 7.5″ × 7.5″ plans @ 1:500 exploring combinations of COV and FAR.
  • Density strategy — four 7.5″ × 7.5″ × 3″ CNC-milled physical models @ 1:500 with a fixed parameter.
  • Topographic coordination — two 7.5″ × 7.5″ pancake models @ 1:200 (Strathmore paper) exploring engagements between buildings and ground.
  • Shading studies — four 7.5″ × 7.5″ 1-point plan perspectives @ 1:5000 with overlapping shadows for different times of year.
  • Material and spatial articulation — 30″ wide × 2–3″ deep physical model @ 1:100 interrelating landforms and built forms.
  • Overall system — 15″ × 15″ CNC-milled physical model @ 1:500; 24″ × 36″ plan @ 1:500; 24″ × 36″ axonometric @ 1:500.
  • Temporal exploration — 15–30 serial sections @ 1:500, and a section perspective @ 1:100 (24″ × 72″) focusing on change through time.

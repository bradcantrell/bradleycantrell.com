Workshop 2 · Programs + Circulation and Vegetal Systems

Program, Circulation,
Vegetal Systems

terrain · social activity · vegetation
Launch: Sept 22Review: Oct 10Program taxonomy · CNC milling · trees

The focus of the second workshop is the relationship between terrain, social activity, and vegetation. It focuses on the social possibilities inherent in the shaping and surfacing of land and landscape — and on individual and collective human behaviors in response to designed and scripted space. As in the previous workshop, terrain is depicted as thick, as catalytic, and as supportive of the intermittent performances of actors across its undulating surfaces. In rendering the section and topography with attributes of performance, the workshop explores the relational processes, energies, and catalysts that give physical form and richness to the landscape.

Program can be understood to interact in three significant ways: to other program, to space, and to time. Rather than operating independently, programs are organized in a taxonomy — groups of dependent land uses organized in a meaningful manner — with specific layouts, dimensions, arrangements, and equipment, and with relationships to topographic requirements (height, depth, aspect, slope, inundation). Continuing in teams of two, beginning with one of the previous models, students develop a programmatic taxonomy and iteratively explore how topography can organize program through space and time. The objective is not to find suitable arrangements of program which suit the initial topography, but to negotiate between land form and use — conflict is productive and can enable rich and varied design responses.

As topography is inflected to suit programmatic needs, two additional systems are folded in: circulation and vegetation. Both have potential to create conflict and tension, but also to link compatible program or buffer incompatible relationships. Their organization is a structuring device, capable of giving legible definition to the programmatic composition and rationalizing adjacencies.

Objectives

  1. To work iteratively through topographic studies that organize — and are in turn organized by — program, circulation, and planting systems.
  2. Develop a program taxonomy documenting compatible land uses in space and time.
  3. Develop an approach to circulation dynamically informed by topographic change and programmatic adjacencies.
  4. Develop an attitude to planting fully imbricated in urbanism, understanding trees and other plantings as living systems with particular habitat requirements.
  5. Understand land use patterns as developing over time in an intentional, spatially dependent, and future-oriented manner.

Workshop Method

The workshop begins with the development of a programmatic taxonomy. An initial obligation to meet a proportional 200% of site area with program must be distributed among a selected list of program; because the total distribution is greater than 100%, this requires strategies to layer program in space, time, and new experimental hybridizations. Sample program can include recreation (sports, leisure, water activities, play, entertainment), light industry (manufacturing, shipping, repair), agriculture/silviculture/aquaculture (greenhouse, farm, compost), commercial (vendors, market, tech), and tourism (docking, lookout).

The taxonomy is developed in tandem with a programmatic timeline (exploring the sequencing of site usage, compatible layering, and fixed events) and a set of iterative programmatic distribution plans, which explore strategies for organizing the distribution of program in space — fixed program asserting itself in space, flexible program accepting blurry zones and overlap. Topography is considered an active design agent: in some instances becoming more regular (forming podiums and excavations), in others more variable. A subset of these plans is then modeled in 3D and produced as CNC-milled physical models (3″ deep white foam), after which circulation and vegetal systems are explored iteratively — trees and paths examined for their ability to unite compatible program, isolate incompatible adjacencies, choreograph sequencing, and give legible definition to anticipated built form.

Workshop Site

The site is the same as in the previous workshop: a portion of the larger South Boston studio site, surrounded by adjacent urban neighborhoods and multi-use districts on all sides except the edge that engages the water. Boston has four distinct seasons, and the programming of the site should account for activity during each; the salinity of the water must be accounted for when organizing trees on site.

Deliverables

  • Programmatic taxonomy — 18″ × 24″ digital drawing; 200% of the site area accounted for with a diversity of program, organized with a clear taxonomy.
  • Programmatic timeline — 18″ × 24″ digital drawing, with attention to overlap and conflict between selected programs.
  • Programmatic distribution — eight 7.5″ × 7.5″ topographic plans @ 1:1000, organizing program in space (fixed vs. flexible).
  • Topographic coordination — four 7.5″ × 7.5″ × 3″ CNC-milled physical models @ 1:1000; two Strathmore paper/pancake models with etching; a 30″ wide × 2–3″ deep physical model @ 1:100 for material articulation.
  • Structure and sequence — eight 7.5″ × 7.5″ topographic plans @ 1:1000 exploring trees and circulation as primary ordering elements.
  • Overall system — 15″ × 15″ × 3″ CNC-milled physical model @ 1:500, and a 24″ × 36″ topographic plan @ 1:500 documenting surface materials and program overlaps in time.
  • Temporal exploration — 15–30 serial sections @ 1:500, and a section perspective @ 1:100 (24″ × 72″) focusing on change through time.

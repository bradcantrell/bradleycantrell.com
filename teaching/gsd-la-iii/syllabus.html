This studio aims to conceptualize and articulate the adaptive city, the city in a state of flux as it responds to changing environmental, programmatic, market, and sociocultural conditions and circumstances. The studio places emphasis on the contingent, the provisional, and the conditional, and asks students to amplify productive instabilities while inventing new types of urban and landscape form.

Landscape offers a distinct starting point for the project of city making. On the one hand, landscape has the ability to address and integrate the multiple environmental, infrastructural, social, and scalar challenges at play in urban design, and can uniquely absorb and hybridize these multiple functions and initiatives in ways that architecture or planning alone cannot. On the other hand, landscape offers operational frameworks and modes of working that emphasize dynamics, change, and improvisation/adaptation — mechanisms that are as much in play in ecosystems as they are in cities.

Consequently, this studio uses landscape both as medium and as mechanism for testing and prototyping ideas on city making. Rather than understanding them as opposites, it emphasizes connections and hybridizations of urban systems and environmental dynamics, and utilizes working methodologies that privilege experimentation and iterative modeling — playful and critical prototyping — that could suggest pathways toward new landscape-based approaches to urban form.

Course Structure

The first part of the studio (first 2/3 of the semester or 10 weeks) is organized as a series of three prototyping workshops that generate organizations of landforms, programmatic assemblies, and distributions of urban forms that can be utilized in defining new types of city districts. These workshops are deliberately experimental: they are intended to develop multiple (rather than singular) starting points (rather than finished forms) for the building blocks of landscapes and cities, and to encourage experimentation, testing, and free play rather than a single inflexible master plan.

The studio workshops are organized topically as follows:

  • Workshop 1: Landforms + Water Flows — how water flows can perform as an active landforming and landscaping agent.
  • Workshop 2: Programs + Circulation and Vegetal Systems — how program distributions, both in space (spacing) and in time (layering), can be used in consonance with circulation and vegetal systems as the primary ordering elements of the city.
  • Workshop 3: Buildings + Public Spaces — how density, defined by FAR (floor area ratio) and coverage, can inform the interrelationship between built form and public space.

The working palette of the studio is focused on particular components in an effort to increase invention and criticality: urban hydrology as a major force in the future design of all coastal cities (rather than all ecological contexts); trees as the traditional major plant life in cities (rather than all forms of plant life); and residential programs as the main building use for stories above the ground floor (rather than all building programs). Each workshop presents different rates of change and notions of temporality — tidal cycles, storm surges, sea level rise, and erosion in Workshop 1; program variability, tree life expectancy, and seasonality in Workshop 2; and solar envelopes, building life expectancy, and economic cycles in Workshop 3.

The second part of the semester (last 1/3 or 5 weeks) is entitled Adaptive Assemblies: Urban Districts, during which students synthesize the prototypes developed during the three workshops. A higher degree of synchronization between elements (landforms, trees, buildings) is expected, while the playful, experimental, exploratory nature of the workshops remains intact — students are asked to resist attempts at comprehensive, totalizing planning strategies and visions. Students work in teams of two throughout the semester.

Course Pedagogy

The course pedagogy is structured according to four principles: formal prototypes as a design process; genealogical iterations as an argumentative process; spatial fields as patterns of occupation; and thick grounds as patterns of density. Prototyping allows for the testing of abstract relationships that can take the form of multiple outcomes when particularized for different contexts — a strategic means for producing a systemic but varied coherence. Genealogical trees register this iterative design process, recording what has happened while pointing toward what could happen, and merging research and design in one thinking structure. Spatial fields — loosely bound aggregates characterized by porosity and local interconnectivity — hybridize the grain of a dense urban deployment with landscape and environmental systems. Thick grounds, designed via excavations and podiums, investigate the effects of density on the ground through the density of activity.

Course Site

The site is located in South Boston, on the coast of Boston Harbor next to the Port of Boston, situated on the south shore of the Reserved Channel, north of E 1st Street, and west of the Conley Container Terminal, extending westward past the water inlet until a virtual continuation of F Street to Drydock Avenue. The site includes brownfields, parking lots, industrial facilities, and several areas of water, all to be considered as land for new development. Bounded by water on its north side and a residential neighborhood on its south edge, it sits between two industrial areas on its east and west limits. The total surface area, including several inlets and the south half of the Reserved Channel, is 190 acres.

The site typifies many found in contemporary cities: leftover, fully dedicated to infrastructure, contaminated to some degree, with bared ecological and hydrologic conditions, bordering on a complex set of residential and institutional neighborhoods, subject to sea level rise, and with increasing pressure for dense urban development. It is a denuded, urbanized, and adapted ecological context — not a natural one. (In Fall 2015, the final exercise instead used Allston Landing along the Charles River.)

Readings

Critical references and background readings are distributed with individual assignments. General and introductory background for the studio:

  • Stan Allen. “Field Conditions.” In Points + Lines: Diagrams for the City. New York: Princeton Architectural Press, 1999. 90–103.
  • Henry F. Arnold. “Principles.” In Trees in Urban Design. New York: Van Nostrand Reinhold, 1980. 39–45.
  • James Corner. “Landscape Urbanism” and “Terra Fluxus.” In Landscape Urbanism: A Manual for the Machinic Landscape (2003) and The Landscape Imagination (2014).
  • Christopher Hight. “Portraying the Urban Landscape.” In Landscape Urbanism: A Manual for the Machinic Landscape. London: Architectural Association, 2003. 22–32.
  • Farshid Moussavi and Alejandro Zaera-Polo. “Phylogenesis: foa’s ark.” In Phylogenesis: Foa's Ark. London; Barcelona: ICA; Actar, 2004. 6–17.
  • Antoine Picon. “Constructing Landscape by Engineering Water.” In Landscape Architecture in Mutation. Zürich: gta Verlag, 2005. 99–115.
  • Chris Reed and Nina-Marie Lister. “Parallel Geneaologies.” In Projective Ecologies. Harvard GSD and ACTAR, 2014. 22–39.
  • Charles Waldheim. “Landscape as Urbanism.” In The Landscape Urbanism Reader. New York: Princeton Architectural Press, 2006. 21–54.
  • Alex Wall. “Programming the Urban Surface.” In Recovering Landscape. New York: Princeton Architectural Press, 1999. 232–249.

Assessment

In Fall 2015, grading comprised four workshop performances (15% each, 60% total), the final review (35%), and attendance/participation including the pre-term workshop (5%). Attendance to studio is mandatory; more than two unjustified absences result in the student being asked to withdraw. Attendance to all reviews in full is mandatory.

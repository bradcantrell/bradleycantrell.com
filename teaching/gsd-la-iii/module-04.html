Adaptive Assemblies
Urban Districts

synthesis · deployment · argument
Launch: Nov 3Final Review: Dec 7South Boston site visit · field trip

Download the full Adaptive Assemblies brief (PDF)

Using landscape as a distinct starting point for the project of city making, this studio aims to conceptualize and articulate the adaptive city, the city in a state of flux as it responds to changing environmental, programmatic, market, and sociocultural conditions and circumstances. The studio places emphasis on the contingent, the provisional, and the conditional, and asks students to amplify productive instabilities while inventing new types of urban and landscape form.

The first part of the studio (first 2/3 of the semester or 10 weeks) was organized as a series of three prototyping workshops generating organizations of landforms, programmatic assemblies, and distributions of urban forms. The second part of the semester (last 1/3 or 5 weeks) is entitled Adaptive Assemblies: Urban Districts. During this stage, students synthesize the prototypes developed during the three workshops. A higher degree of synchronization between elements (landforms, trees, buildings) is expected, while the playful, experimental, exploratory nature of the workshops remains intact — students should resist attempts at comprehensive, totalizing planning strategies and visions.

Four Questions

Topic vs. Method

While the first three workshops were highly methodological (encouraging competency), the last portion of the studio places emphasis on the topic of each project (enabling radicality) — aspiring to produce a radical project that is competently grounded. What is the thesis of your proposed urban district? How is adaptability defined in your project? How is landscape used as a medium for imagining and enabling a new type of urbanity?

Position vs. Implementation

The intellectual position from which you are approaching the disciplinary question of city making needs to be clearly defined and argued for. What landscapes and cities can be considered precedents for your project? What design decisions shape and implement your vision? What constitutes rigor in the context of your project — and what kind of urbanism are you arguing for?

Abstract Swath vs. Urban Site vs. Global Prototype

The studio has followed an inverted trajectory to the usual one — working on iterative prototypes first and on the urban site second, to emphasize risk, creativity, and imagination. This portion of the studio is the opportunity to fully engage with the studio site. How does your proposed urban assembly engage with the existing and surrounding urban environment? How is your project a prototype for a global condition — in which other sites could it take place? How does duration influence how your prototype engages the existing environment?

Development vs. Presentation

In this second part of the semester, how your project is presented is your responsibility. What is the image of this urban district? What representation techniques are most appropriate for representing adaptability and temporality? What documents are necessary — and give a title to your project.

Objectives

  1. To develop a landscape-based strategy for framing new urban development on a leftover urban site.
  2. To develop more specific and detailed relationships between landscape, landform, and building form across a strip of proposed urban fabric.
  3. To elaborate on the environmental, hydrological, social, programmatic, spatial, infrastructural, and experiential informants of the detailed urban assemblies.
  4. To argue for the proposed urban assembly through techniques of iteration and critical comparison.
  5. To argue for a particular kind of urbanism through the ways by which the proposed urban assembly is drawn and modeled.

Method

Deploying the prototypes. Describe how the urban strategy developed in the projects can be applied to the whole site — not a comprehensive plan, but the main guiding factors of the strategy as the means to describe how the prototype could occupy the whole site, focusing on precursors and triggers. Depict the large-scale flows and frameworks (circulatory, hydrologic, ecological, phenomenal) that inform how the project can be deployed, demonstrating flows and intensities as well as territorial staking strategies, and emphasizing phasing, sequencing, temporality, and adaptation.

Sharpening the thesis. Continue to synthesize the different layers of the urban assembly while radicalizing the argument. The genealogical tree should help defend the proposed urban fabric by framing precedents, strategies, and temporal variations; give a title to the project that summarizes its ambitions.

Planning the presentation. Set up final requirements using the deliverables list below as a guideline.

Final Review Deliverables

The following is a baseline of the type, amount, and variety of deliverables expected at the final review. Students are expected to produce their own list based on their projects, in coordination with their instructors — with a full set of labels, scale notations, legends, and dimensions, using color and notation systems selectively and consistently.

  • Genealogical tree(s) — 94″ × 70″ (one rolling board) digital drawing, interrelating all iterations generated throughout the semester.
  • Hydrological, ecological, programmatic timeline — describing all the cycles considered in the project.
  • Site research — digital drawing @ 1:10,000, contextual plan diagramming important regional systems.
  • Generative framework map-plan — digital drawing @ 1:5,000 or 1:2,000, depicting large-scale flows and frameworks.
  • Global prototype — multiple diagrams depicting the conditions necessary for the kind of urbanism proposed, and other qualifying sites.
  • Programmatic, vegetal, and building taxonomies — digital drawing.
  • Catalogs of landscape, programmatic, and building components — multiple axonometric drawings with annotated dimensions, densities, use, FAR, and environmental factors.
  • Iterative models — multiple 7.5″ × 7.5″ and 15″ × 15″ physical models from across the semester.
  • Studies — topographic, planting, programmatic studies @ 1:1,000; density and shading studies @ 1:500.
  • Material and spatial articulation — three 30″ wide × 2–3″ deep physical models @ 1:100, plus a final 30″ wide × 10″ deep sectional model @ 1:100.
  • Overall system — physical model(s) @ 1:500 (24″ × 36″, or three 15″ × 15″); 36″ × 70″ axonometric and plan @ 1:500 or 1:200; three 6″ × 36″ sections @ 1:500 or 1:200.
  • Temporal exploration — 15–30 serial sections @ 1:500; section perspective @ 1:100 (24″ × 72″); oscillation of the overall system as an animation with screenshots.
  • Illustrative vignettes — three section perspectives @ 1:100 (12″ × 36″); three sectional details @ 1:50; simulations and/or animations (multiple video clips).

Site Visit

As part of this last portion of the studio, students visit the site: leftover, fully dedicated to infrastructure, contaminated to some degree, with bared ecological and hydrologic conditions, bordering on a complex set of residential and institutional neighborhoods, subject to sea level rise, and with increasing pressure for dense urban development — a denuded, urbanized, and adapted ecological context, not a natural one.

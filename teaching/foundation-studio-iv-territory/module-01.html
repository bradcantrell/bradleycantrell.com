"As a creative practice, mapping precipitates its most productive effects through a finding that is also a founding; its agency lies in neither reproduction nor imposition but rather in uncovering realities previously unseen or unimagined, even across seemingly exhausted grounds. Thus, mapping unfolds potential; it re-makes territory over and over again, each time with new and diverse consequences."

James Corner — "The Agency of Mapping"

Module I seeks to unpack the issues framed in the studio introduction through simultaneous research, mapping, and modeling. More than simply collecting research to document existing conditions, research as defined in this module should focus on expansion and synthesis. Expansion might involve mapping across multiple site scales and projections; research along multiple timescales to reveal past, present, and future states; or indexing along a vertically elongated section to reveal geological and atmospheric conditions. Synthesis requires a research approach that establishes hypotheses to test, questions to answer, and conclusions to challenge — this research is not neutral; rather, it should tell a particular story about the Central Valley that lays a foundation for future strategic investigation.

It is critical in this phase that your research is not restricted to planimetric maps and diagrams. Rather, you should develop research utilizing a variety of metrics and formats including maps, diagrams, sections, visualizations, writing, and models. Modeling in particular will serve as a primary medium in this studio.

Objectives

The objectives of this module are threefold:

  1. to become familiar with the Central Valley through a variety of lenses
  2. to establish the iterative and experimental methodology of the studio
  3. to develop a manifesto outlining a strategic approach for future intervention

Part I: Research + Mapping

Working in groups of three, students should select one lens from each of the three groups in the list below. Together, these lenses should help you to form a perspective through which to view the complex and challenging nature of the Central Valley, and ultimately to frame strategies for targeted intervention. For each lens, students should gather topical maps, datasets, texts, and other primary sources, with a focus on three scales: 1) the entire Central Valley, 2) one or more of its five hydrological regions, and 3) your group's designated hydrological subregion. To frame a narrative, focus on one or more catalytic events (fire, flood, drought, subsidence) occurring in that subregion.

Socio-Cultural Lenses

1. History — historical photos, aerials, timelines, important events at multiple timescales, including geological, paleontological, and anthropological.

2. Culture — holidays, celebrations, cultural practices of immigrant and second-generation communities, the importance of water and landscapes in these practices.

3. Society — current demographics and demographic shifts, countries of origin for immigrant populations, population dynamics, age groups.

Economic Lenses

4. Economy — poverty, agricultural and industrial revenue, federal incentive funding, from both micro (individual/company) and macroeconomic (state/nation/global) scales.

5. Technology — patents, key agricultural and hydrological technologies, monitoring and sensing technologies.

6. Productivity — agricultural practices and history, major crops and historic yields, federal incentives and subsidies, major agricultural industries and players.

Ecological Lenses

7. Hydrology — water bodies and systems (especially as these have changed over time), dams and hydrological infrastructure, groundwater, historic rainfall and droughts, seasonal patterns.

8. Ecology — flora/fauna, impacts of drought, subsidence and erosion, and agriculture on the region's ecology.

9. Geology — topography, topographic shifts, geological history, soils, subsidence.

Part 2: Modeling

In addition to gathering research across the entire Central Valley, student groups will create a topographic model of a single watershed from within the region. Together, these models will form a large-scale physical model of the entire Central Valley which will serve as proving grounds for a series of "live models" to be produced in Module II.

Deliverables

  1. Atlas: a refined print publication that synthesizes the research and mapping through your group's lenses. The atlas should be cohesively designed, and should include a variety of maps, diagrams, and accompanying text.

  2. Model: a physical topographic model of your group's watershed/basin, milled in high density foam.

  3. Manifesto: a 30 second video that outlines your group's research hypothesis and lays forth your group's approach, conclusions, and recommendations. This is NOT a mere summary of research — the manifesto should be bold, provocative, and forward-thinking.

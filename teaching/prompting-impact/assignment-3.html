The Score

❯ cat brief.mdthe premise

Assignment 1 read your reach. Assignment 2 sensed it, and taught you to keep the disagreements between your sources on the page instead of settling them. Both were about knowing. This one is about daring something.

You will make a proposition for your 2 km, and it will not be a plan. A plan draws the finished thing and asks the river to hold still while it is built. Even dammed, this river does not hold still. Lawrence Halprin wrote scores instead, instructions that set up a performance and leave room for the performers to do something no one drew in advance. Your performers are the water, the sediment, the species, the TVA release schedule and the people who use this bank. You are writing the rules they play by.

Assignment 3 writes the score, has the machine perform it, and connects it to your neighbors. Eighteen scores on one river make a single corridor proposition, and the corridor map will show where they reinforce each other and where they collide.

❯ step 0 · the operatorthe machine as co-creator

In the first two assignments the model was an instrument you audited. Here it is also a collaborator that generates options, more and stranger than you would draw yourself, including images. You still direct it, and you still audit every contribution. The ledger does not stop at the data. It runs through the design. In this assignment the ledger lives in two places, the last section of your score.md and the grounded and speculative marks in proposition.geojson.

The three rules from Assignment 2 still hold. Paste the file. Ask for the file, not the values. Make it show its source. Two more apply here.

4
Ask for the version you would never draw
The first answer a model gives is the most probable one, which means it is the most generic one. Ask for the strange version on purpose, then cultivate one piece of it. A cut to the stool of a coppiced tree, the stump it regrows from, is a provocation, not an instruction, and the tree answers with something no one specified. Use the model the same way.
5
Every image carries its prompt
Keep the prompt history for every image you use. What you asked, what came back, what you changed and why. An image without its history cannot go in the ledger, and an image that is not in the ledger cannot go on the map.
❯ step 1 · write the scorethree to five rules

Start a file called score.md now. Every step below adds to it, and it is one of the three things you hand in.

Begin with your one question and with what Assignment 2 taught you about this reach, especially the disagreement you found between your own layer and an instrument. That gap is often the best place to put a rule, because it is where your reach is doing something no single source describes.

Write one line of starting moves, what you change on day one. They are all the untended future in step 2 gets, so make them count. Then write three to five rules. Each rule has three parts.

1
A trigger
A condition one of your instruments can read. When knot1 stage passes 820 ft. When the Open-Meteo forecast first drops below freezing. When iNaturalist records a heron within the quad. If no instrument on your panel can see the trigger, name the one you would add and mark the rule speculative. A rule whose trigger your panel can read is grounded.
2
An operation
What happens. Cut, plant, open, release, close a path, let a bank erode, stop mowing, do nothing on purpose. Operations, not outcomes. The rule says what you do. The river decides what it becomes.
3
A place
Where on your quad the operation happens, drawn on the map as a point, line or area. Draw it in geojson.io over your aerial, the same way you traced your own layer in Assignment 2, add a property called rule with its number, and save it as GeoJSON. Every rule has a geometry, and every geometry stays inside your bounds.
A score is not a plan in disguise. If your rules only make sense when they produce one particular drawing, you have written a plan and called it a score. Test each rule by asking what it would do in a year you did not expect. A good rule still means something in that year.
Feed the modelPaste your one question, your meta.json and the disagreement paragraph from your source assessment. Ask for ten candidate rules, each with a trigger your panel can read, and ask for three of them to be ones you would not have thought of. Use any of the ten, develop your own, or both. Keep only the ones you can defend, and rewrite every one you keep in your own words.
❯ step 2 · perform itthree conditions, three futures

A score is only known by its performances. Have the model perform yours three times, under three conditions, and make one future from each.

The model does not know your river. It has no hydraulics, no sediment budget and no record of what lives in your quad. Left alone it will tell a fluent story about a river like yours, and that story is the same generic picture the image models make, only in words. What it can do is reason from the files you give it. So treat the performance as a test of your rules. Are they clear enough to be performed, and what do they set in motion that you did not see coming? You decide whether the river would actually do that here.

conditionwhat it asks
an ordinary yearThe river as the gauge usually reports it, regulated, seasonal, unremarkable. Your data/ folder holds days of stage readings, not a year, so treat any stage below the action level of 817 ft as ordinary. What do your rules do when nothing dramatic happens?
a flood yearknot1 at major stage, 830 ft. Moderate is 825, minor is 820 and action is 817. If your data holds a past rise, use how fast it came up. Which rules fire, in what order, and what does the bank look like when the water goes down?
fifty years untendedYour starting moves, then no one comes back. No one is there to carry out a rule, so this future shows what your starting moves do on their own. Start from the species iNaturalist records in your quad and the land cover under it. What does the reach build on its own from what you set in motion?

Each future is three things together. The narration of the performance, which rules fired and what happened. An image of the reach in that condition, generated or drawn. And the drawing of the operation that produced it, a section, a plan diagram or a sequence showing which rules fired and what they did. Draw it yourself, by hand or in any tool. A clear sketch is enough. Read the narration the way you read a source in Assignment 2, and for each future write down what your data can hold up and what you are choosing to imagine. Those become the grounded and speculative lines in proposition.geojson. Keep each narration with its future in your score.md. The prompts behind it go in your ledger.

No image without its operation. Image models are very good at a particular picture, lush and sunlit and vaguely sustainable, that could be anywhere. That picture says nothing about this reach. An image earns its place only when it sits beside the drawing of the rules that made it, and when something in it could only be true of your 2 km.
Feed the modelPaste the files, not just the score. Your score and the condition, your meta.json, the files in your data/ folder, your own layer and your disagreement paragraph. Do this once for each condition. Ask it to narrate the performance before it draws anything. Which rule fires first, what the water does, what moves, what grows. Ask it to name the file behind every event in the narration, and to mark as asserted anything it cannot trace to one. Then ask what it would need to know to perform your rules properly that is not in these files. That list is the speculation in your future. It is also a good start on the second question in step 3. Then ask for the image from that narration, not from your title. If your model cannot make images, take the narration to one that can, or draw it yourself. Ask for the version that surprises you, and keep the prompt history.
❯ step 3 · choosea hypothesis in spatial form

Now judge your own performances. Name the future you would cultivate and the one you would design against. The first becomes the cultivates value in proposition.geojson, and both go in your score.md. Then answer three questions in a few sentences each.

1
Resist, recover or transform
Is your score built to hold the reach as it is, to bring it back quickly after disturbance, or to let it become something new? Say which, and say what that costs.
2
What would tell you
Which instrument, on your panel or one you would add, would show which of your three futures is actually unfolding, and how long it would take to see it. This is what makes the proposition a hypothesis rather than a wish.
3
Fit for whom
Who and what does this reach serve under your score, and who or what bears the cost? A heron, a mussel bed, a person fishing from the bank, the dam operator, the neighbor downstream.
Feed the modelPaste your three futures and your choice. Ask it to argue for the future you would design against. What a future could be good for is something it can find. Which one you cultivate is yours to decide.
❯ step 4 · the corridorgives downstream, asks upstream

Your reach does not end at its bounds. Water, sediment, species, people, debris, even temperature all cross the line. Site numbers run with the river, so the reach above yours is the number before yours and the reach below is the number after. Site 1 asks the Holston and the French Broad. Site 18 gives to the river beyond the corridor.

Write two lines.

↓
Gives downstream
What your score sends to the next reach. Sediment released in a flood year. Cooler water from a shaded bank. A seed source. A trail that continues.
↑
Asks upstream
What your score needs from the reach above. Clear water, or sediment. A corridor for a species to arrive along. A bank that does not send debris at the wrong moment.

Each line has three parts, what crosses the line, when it crosses, and the sentence itself in your words.

For what, choose one of water, sediment, species, people, debris or temperature. Species covers seeds and fish as well as birds, and people covers a path or a trail. For when, choose one of ordinary year, flood year, fifty years untended or always. The first three are the conditions from step 2, and always is for something that crosses every year. The short list is how the corridor map finds your neighbor. The sentence is how we know what you meant.

Once the Assignment 2 outcomes page goes up after Thursday, November 12, open the maps of the reach above you and the reach below you and read what each one is doing. You do not have to agree with them. Your ask is a guess about the reach above, and your give is a guess about the reach below. On the corridor map, every place where one reach gives what the next one asks for will show as a link, and every place where they collide will show as a conflict. A conflict is not a mistake. It is the corridor telling us something no single reach could.

❯ step 5 · export it and map itproposition.geojson

Everything so far goes into one file in a shared shape, proposition.geojson. Your own map reads it, and so does the corridor map. This is how eighteen scores become one corridor.

Images live on your site. Put each image and drawing in your repo or folder, no wider than 1600 pixels. The file holds the full https URL of each one. An image that only exists in a chat window will not be on the corridor map.

    "starting_conditions": "what you change on day one",
    "stance": "transform",
    "cultivates": "flood year",
    "tells_me": "the instrument and reading that would show which future is unfolding",

    "asks_upstream": { "what": "sediment", "when": "always",
                       "line": "no new riprap on the bank just upstream, so sediment keeps arriving" },
    "futures": [
      { "condition": "ordinary year", "image": "https://…/future-1.jpg", "drawing": "https://…/future-1-section.png",
        "grounded": "what in it comes from your data", "speculative": "what you are choosing to imagine" },
      { "condition": "flood year", … },
      { "condition": "fifty years untended", … }
    ]
  },
  "features": [
    { "type": "Feature",
      "geometry": { "type": "Polygon", "coordinates": [ [[lon, lat], …] ] },

}

Do not type this file by hand. Every coordinate comes from the geometry you drew in step 1, exported as GeoJSON. Paste the example above, your meta.json, the GeoJSON of your drawn rules and your score.md, and ask for proposition.geojson.

Read the files pasted below and write proposition.geojson for my corridor map.

  rules
  follow the example's shape exactly, same keys, same nesting
  every coordinate pair is [longitude, latitude]
  take geometry only from my drawn GeoJSON, never invent or move a point
  every feature must fall inside the bounds in my meta.json

  starting_conditions is my starting moves

  cultivates is one of ordinary year, flood year, fifty years untended

  what is one of water, sediment, species, people, debris, temperature
  when is one of ordinary year, flood year, fifty years untended, always
  line is my corridor sentence, word for word

  files

  <my drawn rules as GeoJSON>

Then check three things before you trust it. Open the file in geojson.io and see that your rules land on your quad and nowhere else. Read every text field and see that the words are yours. After you update your site, open each image URL in a private window and see that it loads.

Save the file into your data/ folder. Then add the proposition to the map you built in Assignment 2, at the same URL. One new layer toggle, proposition, shows your rules as geometry on the quad. A viewer shows your three futures, each image beside its drawing. Both read data/proposition.geojson, so your map shows exactly what you hand in. Keep your Assignment 2 layers. The proposition sits on top of what you learned, it does not replace it.

Feed the modelPaste your map's index.html and your proposition.geojson. Ask for the proposition layer first and the viewer second, one change at a time, and ask which lines it touched. Both should load data/proposition.geojson rather than a copy of what is in it.

❯ cat hard.mdthe trap

The temptation is to fall in love with one beautiful future and write the rules backwards from it. That is a plan, and even with the dams holding it, this river keeps moving out from under plans.

The harder and better work is to write rules you believe in and then be genuinely curious about what they do, including the futures you did not want. A score that only ever produces the picture you hoped for has not been performed. It has been rendered.

Be daring in the rules and honest in the ledger. The creativity is in what you set in motion. The rigor is in saying, every time, which parts of the future the data can hold up and which parts are yours.

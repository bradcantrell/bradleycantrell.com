Introduction

Meeting Times: Wednesday 12:00–14:30.

Teaching Faculty: Michael Lee, Reuben M. Rainey Professor in Landscape History; Bradley Cantrell, Professor and Chair.

Contemporary Berlin is a thriving metropolis, with a creative energy almost unmatched among European capitals. The seat of the federal government of Germany since 1999, it has undergone a significant identity shift as development and gentrification have come increasingly in conflict with the city’s postwar legacy as a center for bohemian life, alternative culture, and experimental art. Described in 2001 by its then mayor Klaus Wowereit as “poor but sexy,” Berlin has subsequently experienced the positive results of rising employment and better public services, but now finds itself trying to manage the complexities of a real estate boom driven by an influx of technology-based companies and investment by international corporations.

Because of its unusually complex history over the past two hundred years, Berlin is a physically fragmented city that can be difficult to read. Unlike most European capitals, it is polycentric in configuration with a shifting fabric of juxtaposed fragments, often stemming from three or four distinct periods. Equally characteristic are significant absences that can only be perceived through knowledge of what was once there. As a result, Berlin presents a singular laboratory for learning to interpret multilayered urban landscapes, but one in which the layers are read not by the methodic excavation of clearly delineated strata but by retrieving and reassembling shards produced by upheaval and cataclysm.

The seminar is organized chronologically in order to make this process of retrieval and reassembly more legible. We will examine Berlin’s urban landscape at many scales, ranging from individual monuments and buildings to urban ensembles, public parks, fallow sites, and infrastructural systems, along with the social and political topographies that shaped them. By charting the city’s growth and expansion over the last two hundred years, we will together construct a framework for representing and making sense of its complex contemporary reality, as well as the dynamics of its increasingly multicultural population.

Course Expectations

  • Weekly attendance is required.
  • Students will prepare the readings and film screenings ahead of time.
  • Students will prepare each week for the role assigned for structured discussions.

Learning Objectives

  • Develop historical and spatial research skills within a digital humanities framework.
  • Expand analytic skills through close readings of texts, films, and images.

Structured Discussions

Each session will include a structured discussion led by students and moderated by the instructors. Students will be assigned specific roles for the discussion, and the roles will be rotated from week to week.

Research Assignment · Urban Atlas of Berlin

Each student will create a digital humanities project that analyzes one of Berlin’s significant urban configurations or landscapes. The sites will be assigned by the instructors from a predetermined list. Using ArcGIS StoryMaps as a platform, students will curate and synthesize a range of materials collected during their research, including historic maps, primary texts, photographs, drawings, video, and other items suitable for their analysis. The project should contain a timeline, clear navigation, and a series of brief original texts that interpret and contextualize the material assembled.

The thematic emphases of each project will vary according to site, but students are encouraged to explore and foreground the socio-ecological dimensions of their case studies when appropriate.

The individual projects will be linked to a central webpage, and will serve collectively as a first sketch for an Urban Atlas of contemporary Berlin.

Final Project submission: 30 November.

To get a sense of the general scope of digital humanities work, we encourage you to consult the following project for ideas: Enchanting the Desert (Stanford University).

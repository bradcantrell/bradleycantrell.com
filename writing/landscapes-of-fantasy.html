2008

Landscapes of Fantasy: Developing Representation Techniques Through Literary Narrative

Publication / Pedagogical Research
Writing
Location
Penn State University, State College, Pennsylvania
Medium
Paper, seminar curriculum (LA 2101 Representation Seminar)
Publication
Proceedings of the Council of Educators in Landscape Architecture (CELA) 2008 Conference

Landscapes of Fantasy introduced literary narrative as the structuring device for advanced digital representation instruction. Rather than teaching software skills through the replication of known visual forms, the seminar asked students to represent environments that existed only in text, displacing them from familiar landscapes into the fictional territories of novels such as Kim Stanley Robinson's Red Mars.

Abstract

The description of landscapes in literature requires the articulation of a broad range of elements that converge to create a clear, contextually rich narrative. This paper explores the introduction of literary narrative and imagination as a subject to shift the contextual framework for digital representation instruction. The incorporation of literary narrative provides a framework for a landscape representation pedagogy that encourages the imagination of rich landscapes developed through successive exercises that focus on the object's relation to the field.

Overview

Pedagogy focusing on landscape representation addresses two facets in student learning: technical skills and the expression of environmental experience. Literature provides a descriptive model that imagines landscape within a larger narrative framework that drives story, character, and environment. Landscape representation primarily focuses on the representation of the field or environment rather than the idealization of the object and thus concerns itself with the expression of experience. Current modeling and rendering software inherently heightens object fetishism with toolsets to construct complex surfaces and develop renderings of hyper-real materials.

Shifting the focus of representation from object to field requires an external framework for reconceptualizing landscape as environment while concurrently developing a cognitive process that displaces ideals and norms. Examining the description of landscape as narrative rather than duplicating known visual forms enables students to imagine landscape as encompassing, experiential, and thus environmental. In the LA 2101 Representation Seminar at the Louisiana State University School of Landscape Architecture, students are introduced to a series of projects focusing on the imagined environment described within literature, an example would be the world of Red Mars, as described by the author Kim Stanley Robinson. The course focuses on the "dematerialization of the architectural (or landscape) object, giving rise to increasing attention to process and context" (Tierney). This examination involves the representation of landscape through a series of exercises that explore device, surface, environment (atmosphere), motion, and process. The students are introduced to techniques derived from historical methods and contemporary visual effects production.

This methodology produces a cohesive framework that students use to guide the construction of each representation process. The representation of specific elements of the landscape is situated within the literature's narrative. The narrative begins to produce a holistic approach to representational processes that is independent of known landscapes. As students are pushed beyond their known world they are asked to re-imagine landscape components as constructors of environment.

Methodology

Landscape representation intends to go beyond the illustration of the object or component. "The perspective image, both through the device of framing and momentary view, is negatively described as a depleted drawing without situation in place" (Clarke). The representation of landscape intends to capture moments or sequences of human experience within an environment or context. Therefore framing a sequence of experience is paramount in representing landscapes. Experience is a term full of connotation, therefore in this discussion its definition will be expressed as observation of some thing or event that conveys knowledge to the subject. Because experience is primarily contextual and defined through personal cognition, interpretation is widely varied. This spectrum of experience, varied and personal, is essential to tap into for meaningful methods of design representation.

Narrative is a construct to develop a focus for landscape objects and experience, creating a cohesive representation schema. Narrative is "considered as one technique for recapitulating experience, in particular, a technique of constructing narrative units which match the temporal sequence of that experience" (Labov and Waletzky). These narrative units describe ranges of time that develop experiential qualities, and this can directly or indirectly correlate with macro or micro scale processes within the landscape. Because there is a lack of correlation in temporal ranges for experience, environmental process, and representation mode, it is important to critically determine how these ranges interact with one another. Narrative can often function in two ways, a vision of events or a culmination of experiences. In either sense inserting narrative as a framework for landscape representation allows students to re-contextualize landscape perceptions.

Implementation

The Louisiana State University Robert Reich School of Landscape Architecture advanced digital representation seminar and lab tackles a number of representational issues that are both technical and imaginative. The course has been taught in two ways, with the introduction of a literary novel as an encompassing framework and with separate passages that drive individual projects. This can be deemed as holistic or delimited, with a range of expressions. Examining both methods allows for a comparison of student involvement, comprehension, and development of technical skills.

Framework

In any digital representation course, instructors and students must always overcome the necessity to teach the fundamental skills necessary to operate software. This includes teaching specific functions and application in order to create specific effects with desirable outcomes. For any digital technology the amount of information necessary to operate the software can be daunting and is beyond the scope of a single seminar and in most cases beyond anyone's career ambitions. In reality there is an over abundance of information available on the internet on techniques and effects, which requires collating in order to organize tutorials and tips that are relevant to the design professions. Beyond existing information students often serendipitously find new ways of manipulating or tweaking the software in order to develop new methods and techniques.

It is therefore necessary to build a repository of information that is relevant to landscape representation using the tools introduced within the seminar. This can be created using a blog, wiki, or other collaborative software environment. At the Louisiana State University School of Landscape Architecture this is accomplished using a collaborative teaching platform called Moodle, which allows students access to simple discussion boards created for specific courses. This archive of information builds itself over the course of the semester through specific assignments that ask students to post based on outlined topics and serendipitous findings.

Two methods of implementing narrative as a pedagogical framework have been tested with similar results. One method entails using an overarching epic or narrative with selected passages to guide assignments. A second method requires students to develop a narrative framework within groups and use the framework as a lens to direct abstract passages applied to each project.

Project Typologies

Diorama: Scene. In order to introduce students to the concept of the virtual set, students are asked to create a diorama composed of elements created with images mapped to planes. Each scene is composed within a single camera movement over the course of fifteen seconds. The diorama functions as a device to portray landscape as a spatial experience. The diorama provides an opportunity to create a landscape representation that is formulated around a limited vantage point. The vantage point and the composition of the diorama is vital to create a compelling experience. Students develop their landscape from a single point of view, focusing environmental definition through user perception.

The diorama serves as a medium for composition, elements placed within space creating an environment from a single vantage point. The diorama is described as a moment, a composition with specific aesthetic and experiential goals. Students are introduced to Daguerre's diorama, which was a theatrical experience allowing patrons to experience a landscape painting that would change subtly through the alteration of light and layering of screens. This dynamic composition was viewed from a single vantage point, similar to current methods of digital representation that are typically presented through projection or digital displays.

Object: Device. Typically the focus of modeling is upon the object or device. A myriad of tools are available to designers that allow for the creation of surfaces and edges to represent a known object. The object can be as simple as a teapot or as complex as a simulated architectural structure. It is important to consider the tools used to represent designed geometries as each directly affects the methods used to generate surfaces. For example, polygon modeling focuses on points, edges, and polygons while NURBS (non-uniform rational b-splines) builds geometry from curves. The manipulation of surfaces happens at the point or edge level for polygons with larger transformations based on larger topological transformations. On the other hand, NURBS modeling transforms larger portions of surfaces through control vertices or hulls and approximates the curves. Students explore multiple methods of surface generation in order to develop a language for digital constructions.

Students develop objects or devices as elements of larger environments, paying specific attention to environmental interface. Typically this is approached with a project that explores a device that directly modifies an existing landscape. The devices focus on functioning, dynamic systems created through multiple elements. In most cases the object may be formed through object instantiation, creating a basis for modularity, or through multiple object typologies. The process of instantiation is explored as a method to create similar objects with multiple transformations that are used to create larger elements. This can be as simple as a building module or multiple plant species that populate an ecosystem.

Environment: Context: Surface. The environment is constructed in several ways and this portion of the process focuses on earth and context. Terrain or earth is expressed digitally through two methods, as surfaces and as volumes. Both methods are generated with similar data: spot elevations, DEM files, and/or isolines. Based on passages that describe an environment or landscape, students generate modified terrain models as surfaces and volumes. The volumetric models are modified through iterative operations based on specific cut and fill calculations, and this is guided based on multiple translations of the literature and accomplished with Autodesk Civil 3D.

It is also possible to imagine landscape as a single surface, expressed in relationship to the viewer. This is a standard convention for most design drawings such as the orthographic projection, which creates an abstracted view of three-dimensional data. At this stage of terrain modeling it is important for students to understand a surface as a representation of a complex underlying model. This surface can then be illustrated in several different ways in order to express a desired result. The surface is typically modified through geometric transformations in order to express portions of the surface and textured in order to illustrate a material quality.

Context builds on earlier studies in the diorama and situates the terrain beyond the local view. This connection to a larger landscape becomes an iterative process, forcing students to reconcile a surface condition with an external motivator. The context is constructed as a series of billboards updated through external imagery, and the updates are driven by fictional landscapes. Students are forced to respond to each update and alter their surface in order to meet the new constraints.

Dynamics: Interaction. The previous approaches have isolated modes of inquiry in order to focus comprehension of complicated environmental variables. In order to illustrate an imagined landscape it is necessary to understand how object and field interact while also considering the viewer's experience. Students are assigned passages of literature that focus on dynamic environmental processes and interactions. This could be the illustration of moments such as water moving down a plant's stem, the creation of a dust storm, or the morning dew burning off as the sun rises. The students use the concept of scene, object, and surface in order to generate a thirty-second animation of the proposed process.

This animation asks students to critically examine how time is framed in a limited representational medium through compression or expansion. The temporal space of animation also requires rethinking composition through time; it is no longer a single frame but instead objects and surfaces are coming to life and the camera must be used to highlight or disregard moments. The final result attempts to expose a complex process from a defined perspective.

Outcome

This work is important as a methodology to introduce complex representation systems that challenge the pictorial. Students recognize the role of each representational element and its context within complex systems and the larger environment. Students also develop a conceptual framework to translate imagination or fantasy to represented spaces, allowing for testing, evaluation, and communication.

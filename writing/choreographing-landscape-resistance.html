"Cyborg Ecology: Choreographing Landscape Resistance" proposed that the boundary between designed landscape and natural ecology had become theoretically and materially untenable, that contemporary territorial conditions are better understood as cyborg ecologies in which biological processes, computational sensing, and infrastructural actuation operate as a single system rather than as separable domains. The essay developed the concept of "choreographing resistance," designing not the form of a landscape but the conditions under which biological and computational agents interact, resist, and co-produce spatial outcomes. Drawing on Haraway's cyborg theory, the essay extended the cyborg concept from individual bodies to territorial systems, arguing that the delta, the constructed wetland, and the sensored watershed are all cyborg ecologies, assemblages in which the boundary between organism, machine, and infrastructure is not blurred but constitutively absent.

Fundamental Questions

Cyborg Ecology addressed fundamental questions about the ontology of designed landscapes: If the boundary between the natural and the designed has become untenable — if contemporary territorial conditions are characterized by the inseparability of biological processes, computational sensing, and infrastructural actuation — then what is a landscape? And what does it mean to design one?

The essay proposed the concept of cyborg ecology as a framework for understanding landscapes in which living systems and technological systems operate as a single integrated entity rather than as separate domains. Drawing on Haraway's cyborg theory and contemporary examples of sensor-driven ecological management, Cyborg Ecology argued that landscape architecture must develop new conceptual tools for working with territories that are neither wild nor domesticated but exist in a condition of ongoing, technologically-mediated negotiation.

This work opened questions about agency, intention, and responsibility: If landscapes are co-produced by algorithms, sensors, and biological processes, who — or what — is designing? And what forms of care are appropriate to systems whose behavior emerges from the interaction of multiple, partially-autonomous agents?

Full Chapter Text

The condition of the Anthropocene is one of ecological hybridities, an evolved state where constructed megaliths exist within noisy gradients of the pseudo natural. These landscapes, the hybridized territories, are the product of the exertion of civil engineering, environmental management, and the pressures of urbanization. Where does the discipline of landscape architecture stake its footing in the ecologically complex and technologically abstract realm of contemporary society? To confront the intractable issues of the Anthropocene it is necessary to unpack the technologies that operate at the scale of the territory. Therefore, the interface between the built environment and the evolving biological and geological systems requires a blurring of strategies that address nascent forms of simulation, sensing, logistics, biological engineering, and technological interface to question the dichotomy of the constructed and the natural.

It is necessary that design, particularly landscape architecture, takes the stance that modifying the territory requires the application of reflexive and responsive technologies, to form new environmental relationships developing hybrid ecological systems based on methods of simulation, sensing, and feedback. These novel forms of ecology demand a deconstruction of current forms of settlement, infrastructure, and biological systems to understand the constructed environment as a synthesizer of biotic and abiotic processes. The interstices of these new relationships become the medium in which the discipline may then examine new potentials for construction and maintenance as a starting point to propose new infrastructural landscapes. This hybridized ecology is a cyborg, positing that the role of landscape architecture is an implementer of new technologies promoting active and real-time methods of environmental stewardship and management. These methodologies span a range of scales, from micro adjustments of chemical processes to automated construction to regional management and monitoring to global remote sensing systems. Primarily, the concept of a cyborg ecology creates a recursive and/or iterative relationship between computation and biology, as well as other landscape processes.

The discipline (of landscape architecture) has long grappled with the material craft of landscape and its subsequent connection with territorial systems. These shifts in scale are primarily addressed through hints of connectivity, purported moments where the complexity of the territory is drawn into the scale of the human figure. There is little doubt that the rhetoric permeating the discipline states the effectiveness of the landscape architect as the planner of the territory, particularly as it relates to socio-cultural issues, bio-physical properties, and ecological fitness. What becomes harder to decipher is how that stated agency manifests itself on the ground, in actionable and nimble forms that live up to the discourse that has developed around process, indeterminacy, and flux.

While not new, the discipline aims to conceptualize and articulate the territory as an adaptive infrastructure, the landscape in a state of flux as it responds to changing environmental, programmatic, market, and sociocultural conditions. The approach places emphasis on the contingent, provisional, and the conditional, and asks designers to amplify productive instabilities while inventing new types of landscape form. Therefore the landscape, physical and ephemeral, formal and indeterminate, is our starting point for the project of generating and maintaining the territory. Landscape has the ability to address and integrate the environmental, social, and scalar challenges at play in territories. Furthermore, landscape architecture absorbs and hybridizes these multiple functions in ways that engineering, environmental science, or planning alone cannot. On the other hand, landscape offers operational frameworks and modes of integration that emphasize dynamics, change, and adaptation—mechanisms that are in play in ecosystems. It is essential to address landscape both as a medium and as a mechanism for testing and prototyping interventions that reshape the territory. Rather than understanding medium and mechanism as opposites, it is important to emphasize connections and hybridizations of systems and environmental dynamics. This then suggests working methodologies that privilege experimentation and iteration—playful and critical prototyping—that suggest pathways toward new responsive landscape-based approaches to territorial systems.

The planet in the Anthropocene is a diverse and complex landscape, functioning at every scale, crystallized across a static framework of infrastructure, physical and logistical. Canals connecting disparate watersheds, pumps pushing water uphill to defy gravity, pollinators transported for crops, thousands of kilometers of levees, irrigation technology that repatterns regions .. a literal cyborg of contemporary humanity. The infrastructural framework is bold, produced via applied capital to transform the hydrologic, climatic, and ecological regimes of the planet. Its success relies on predictability, models that define the future via abstraction and simplification. The friction unraveling the planet we occupy is situated where the environmental dynamics of territories confront the stasis of an aging technological, political, and social infrastructure. To promote a projective language for territorial landscape intervention, it is necessary to understand the influence that infrastructures impress upon landscape systems. Historically the implementation of landscape infrastructure has relied on a regime fixed by epistemological frameworks. Verifiable truths that are seemingly self evident but also calculated, modeled, and verified as a basis for the proposal of megaliths, infrastructure that is constructed and operates at the scale of the territory. What is unknown is quickly excluded or over engineered in the need for safety and predictability.

Stasis is the defining factor of this framework, set within a viscous and vigorous environment that demands change and erases history. To operate within this region requires the embrace of indeterminacy, calculated failure, and methods to harness feedback. A design strategy that relies on adaptation via prototyping, framing the landscape as a laboratory to test the effects of systems at scale. Prototypes that exist throughout the life of a landscape, a feedback loop, not a quest for hyper-efficiency or control. To have agency in the territory requires tools and strategies that construct an epistemology that is adaptive.

To create an adaptive epistemology for design it is necessary to not only adjust our theoretical frameworks but to also develop new tools for representation, experimentation, and prototyping. Laboratories that focus less on empirical data and instead highlight the methods and interventions required to choreograph dynamic landscapes. These choreographies focus on resistances, producing interventions that disrupt or catalyze ecological systems to produce landscapes that are robust, relying on the concept of anti-fragility. Infrastructures and landscapes that produce a heuristic, evolving alongside one another, testing weaknesses and learning to repair, patch, and grow.

How might we approach design's agency in an adaptive epistemology, through a new set of design tools? A suite of responsive interfaces, interventions, and infrastructures that sculpt, inflect, and choreograph the complexities of ecological systems to develop knowledge that is rooted but adaptive. A body of this work can be found in my own research over the past decade that originally formed during my time researching infrastructural adaptation in the Mississippi River Delta after the devastating effects of Hurricane Katrina in August of 2005. This work attempts to merge the computational prowess of numerical modeling with the tangible tools of physical modeling developed in North America by the Army Corp of Engineers.

The research was realized in the formation of the Responsive Environments and Artifacts Lab (REAL), co-directed with Professor Allen Sayegh, at the Harvard Graduate School of Design. REAL has conducted research that examines the potential of responsive technologies across a variety of scales focusing on systems at the territorial scale and the manipulation of indeterminate land building using real-time sensing, robotics, and adaptive management techniques. The research and lab has recently found a new home at the University of Virginia School of Architecture where a team of academics including myself, Brian Davis, Matthew Seibert, Andrea Hansen, Xun Liu, and Zihao Zhang, are developing the platforms and interfaces for a nascent suite of design tools to confront adaptive design protocols.

Responsive technologies, devices that foster methods of sensing, processing, and actuation, are typically controlled using pre-programmed interactions. A simple example is the use of a thermostat to control the temperature in a building, when the temperature drops below, or goes above, a determined threshold the thermostat automates the starting or stopping of the HVAC system and maintaining a constant temperature. New forms of response are being developed that use deep learning and machine intelligence to form the rules and patterns of interaction. Using the thermostat example, the Nest thermostat programs itself based on patterns of use and optimal energy savings. Using these two goals, the thermostat learns the user's schedule, comfortable temperature range, and develops a pattern that optimizes the heating and cooling regime for optimal energy savings and maximum comfort. The thermostat takes into account energy demand and pricing, the speed at which spaces are cooled, and equilibrium states. This specialized intelligence goes beyond automating the system and instead forms a pattern of automation unique to the learning algorithm it uses and the context in which it functions.

To further explore this example it is helpful to specifically unpack the issue of coastal adaptation. Approximately 40% of people across the globe are living within 100 kilometers of coastal and riverine environments. This means that three-quarters of the world's mega-cities and critical infrastructure are situated next to the ocean and will be required to adapt to fluctuating sea levels over the next century. This adaptation will be a monumental task, requiring huge adjustments in the physical location of cities and the construction of new forms of barriers and mediations through levees, sea walls, and control structures. The research posits that the energy, embodied in the hydrological systems that build coastal lands, can be choreographed to mitigate the effects of sea level rise. The work focuses on sediment transport, the suspension and movement of sediment within the water column and how small modifications to the water system can be used to construct new land. It is also necessary to realize the connections to global sensing systems and models for the construction of coastal environments, particularly how real-time sensing can be used to choreograph in situ construction of coastal landscapes.

The researchers are currently utilizing a geomorphology modeling table, originally designed by Steve Gough at EMRiver, to simulate dynamic riverine and coastal environments, this becomes the site in which designers begin to develop approaches to a fluctuating hydrology. The models utilizes four sensing systems using ultrasonic range-finding sensors, real-time photogrammetry, image analysis, and capacitance sensors to record sediment behavior, surface morphology, and water flow. The geomorphology model is imagined as its own space with dynamic phenomena that is unique to its conditions. This allows the model to be free of attempting to simulate a specific physical landscape or reality. With that being said, there are baseline conditions derived from prototypical geomorphological tropes, such as delta building, meandering rivers, backwater swamp, and tidal zone, among others.

The modeling space creates a tangible environment that can generate a huge range of conditions. These conditions are created using a synthetic sediment of varying sizes and densities, pumps to create stream flow and groundwater flow, and an adjustable bed that can set lateral and longitudinal slope as well as depth. These variables can be altered to set the initial conditions and also controlled over time. The controls on the table allow the researchers to program hydrographs for both stream flow and groundwater flow, creating flow input profiles that are repeatable. The media feeders can create measured mixes of sediment that are inserted into the water flow and there is also an injector that can introduce dye plumes into the flow, helping to visualize the water movement.

The varied synthetic sediment sizes and densities self-organize based on water velocities resembling a range of particulate matter from an abstraction of large gravel, yellow, to a fine silt, red. In coordination with the modeling elements the modeling space also has a sensing system that allows for the researchers to gather data on surface morphology, flow rates, sediment moisture, and image analysis. Using ultrasonic sensors that measure distance, the system can move the sensors across the table to record precise spot elevations as well as continuous sections. A Microsoft Kinect is also used to record a continuously updated point cloud of the surface, combining these it is possible to derive a precise real-time model of the surface at multiple levels of detail.

The controls and sensors allow the model to deliver a broad range of fluvial landscape scenarios with consistent seeding parameters. The overlay of sensing technologies allows for the acquisition of data to experiment with methods of visualization, analysis, and interaction. The model is more complex than static models but it is important to acknowledge that it is an abstraction that generalizes fluvial landscapes. The work advances a form of design research that holds great promise for how we engage large scale environmental change. Engaging highly dynamic systems and utilizing their energy to compose new terrains provides a mechanism for massive change with minimal input. The feedback between sensing systems and landscape modification forms a construction methodology that is tunable and challenges methods of complex simulation and static infrastructure.

Using the real-time digital models, the researchers are then creating devices that respond to the changes in the physical model such as robotic sediment gates, repositionable sieves, and flow disruptors. The intent of this exploration is to develop prototypes and a design language that enables designers to create novel hydrologic landscapes that are tied to sensed phenomena and forecasting models. The current scope of work provides a proof of concept for an emerging toolset to engage territorial landscape systems. This toolset relies on realtime sensing of landscape phenomena coupled with methods of adaptive management to produce closer relationships between landscape analysis and methods of construction.

Conceptually this proposes a method that relies on designing operations that intervene locally but aggregate to produce territorial change. Most importantly this is a form of landscape choreography that continually learns from itself by processing the results of its actions before choosing the next intervention. A continuous call and response between biological systems and an active infrastructure. To produce these interventions, research has taken different approaches, creating responsive prototypes that influence the sediment flow through flow enhancers, resistors, and redirectors. An important step in this work has been to develop a range of motion within the infrastructural interventions and to then document their influence. Using image analysis to record subsequent iterations this documentation illustrates a formal design language that addresses indeterminacy in fluvial landscapes. Producing topographic plans that speak to movement and probabilities of change. The illustrations highlight a landscape form that expresses areas of stasis and flux, forming a gradient of interaction between the two.

The work of Leif Estrada, utilized REAL to explore how the Los Angeles river channel may be deconstructed and reconstituted as a composed and managed river bed within a highly developed urban fabric. The approach utilizes a series of individually controlled insertions into the sediment that redirect the graded material from the demolished channel. The system works with an evolving and cyclical hydrological system to re-pattern the river bed. The new river bed is the product of interactions that guide outcomes, most importantly these outcomes are controlled within a range and their aggregation is a consequence of the current hydrology while projecting a series of future goals.

Ideally, the work produced helps to enhance the toolset of the landscape architect and develop design approaches that utilize our expanding command of computation. It proposes a method of landscape intervention that collapses representation, analysis, documentation, and construction. This requires a tactical approach to landscape formation relying on the design of operations that rest within local observation, sensing and long term forecasting. I would not propose that the product of the work is a representation of the landscapes we want to live in, in fact the work does not address this. The feedback between sensing systems, machine intelligence, and landscape modification creates a construction methodology that is tunable and challenges current methods of complex systems simulation and static infrastructure. The goal is to move beyond known representations and design methods, to develop the tools necessary to address current global conditions and the rhetoric of flux and indeterminacy characterized in the last decade of the profession.

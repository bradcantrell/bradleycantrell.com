“Cyborg Ecology: Choreographing Landscape Resistance” proposed that the boundary between designed landscape and natural ecology had become theoretically and materially untenable, that contemporary territorial conditions are better understood as cyborg ecologies in which biological processes, computational sensing, and infrastructural actuation operate as a single system rather than as separable domains. The essay developed the concept of “choreographing resistance,” designing not the form of a landscape but the conditions under which biological and computational agents interact, resist, and co-produce spatial outcomes. Drawing on Haraway’s cyborg theory, the essay extended the cyborg concept from individual bodies to territorial systems, arguing that the delta, the constructed wetland, and the sensored watershed are all cyborg ecologies, assemblages in which the boundary between organism, machine, and infrastructure is not blurred but constitutively absent.

Fundamental Questions

Cyborg Ecology addressed fundamental questions about the ontology of designed landscapes: If the boundary between the natural and the designed has become untenable — if contemporary territorial conditions are characterized by the inseparability of biological processes, computational sensing, and infrastructural actuation — then what is a landscape? And what does it mean to design one?

The essay proposed the concept of cyborg ecology as a framework for understanding landscapes in which living systems and technological systems operate as a single integrated entity rather than as separate domains. Drawing on Haraway's cyborg theory and contemporary examples of sensor-driven ecological management, Cyborg Ecology argued that landscape architecture must develop new conceptual tools for working with territories that are neither wild nor domesticated but exist in a condition of ongoing, technologically-mediated negotiation.

This work opened questions about agency, intention, and responsibility: If landscapes are co-produced by algorithms, sensors, and biological processes, who — or what — is designing? And what forms of care are appropriate to systems whose behavior emerges from the interaction of multiple, partially-autonomous agents?

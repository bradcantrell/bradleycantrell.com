Adaptive Epistemologies and Neo-Wilds

Glossary

Key Terms and Concepts

Adaptive Epistemologies and Neo-Wilds

—

A

Adaptive Epistemology

A framework for knowledge production that treats design propositions as testable hypotheses deployed in real-world conditions, where learning emerges through iterative cycles of proposal, feedback, and adjustment. Rather than seeking complete predictive understanding before action (predictive control), adaptive epistemology accepts partial knowledge and ongoing uncertainty, generating understanding through engaged practice. See also reflexive stewardship, feedback loops.

Reference: Discussed throughout, particularly Ch. 01, Ch. 02

Adaptive Management

A systematic approach to environmental management that treats management actions as experiments, explicitly testing assumptions and adjusting strategies based on feedback from ecological monitoring. Originating with C.S. Holling, adaptive management emphasizes learning-by-doing in contexts of uncertainty and complexity. See adaptive epistemology for philosophical extension.

Reference: Ch. 01, Ch. 02

Antifragility

Taleb's concept of systems that become stronger through disorder and disruption, rather than merely recovering to baseline (resilience). A system is antifragile if variability and shocks improve its function. Applied to landscape design: systems designed to benefit from unexpected perturbations, not just endure them.

Reference: Ch. 03 (Refractions)

Assemblage

A theoretical concept describing complex arrangements of heterogeneous elements—humans, machines, materials, institutions, ideas—that have no singular origin or master plan but emerge through dynamic interaction. Applied to landscape: territories are assemblages of biology, infrastructure, computation, and culture in constant negotiation.

Reference: Ch. 01, Ch. 05, Ch. 11

—

B

Boundary Object

A concept from STS (Star & Griesemer): an object or representation (map, model, diagram) that can hold different meanings for different communities while still coordinating action across them. Maps function as boundary objects: engineers, ecologists, and designers see the same map but project different questions onto it.

Reference: Ch. 05 (Tools)

—

C

Cultivant / Cultivation

Extending Raxworthy's "viridic," a mode of stewardship that neither seeks total control nor complete abandonment, but rather tending and actively supporting biological autonomy while shaping conditions. Cultivation acknowledges human participation without domination. See also viridic.

Reference: Ch. 01, Ch. 05, Ch. 11

Cybernetics / Cybernetic Systems

The study of control and communication in living organisms and machines. A cybernetic system monitors its own behavior through feedback and adjusts to maintain stability or reach goals. Pask, Beer, and Ashby are key figures. Applied to landscape: recognizing that ecosystems are self-regulating cybernetic systems, not inert resources awaiting human direction.

Reference: Ch. 09 (Interactions), Ch. 10 (Generational Robots)

—

D

Datum

The reference point or baseline against which other measurements are taken. In topographic surveying, the datum is the horizontal reference (sea level, a benchmark). Conceptually: what you choose as your measuring stick determines what becomes visible. Changing the datum reveals a different landscape.

Reference: Ch. 03, Ch. 05

Data Visualization / Visualization

The process of representing information graphically. Conceptually treated here not as neutral presentation but as argument—every choice of scale, color, perspective, and what to include or exclude shapes what viewers can know and believe about the phenomenon represented.

Reference: Ch. 05 (Tools), Ch. 06 (Models)

—

E

Epistemic Authority / Epistemic Justice

Concepts from Miranda Fricker regarding who has the right and power to be heard as a knower. Epistemic injustice occurs when someone's testimony or knowledge is dismissed or devalued based on prejudice against their social position. Applied to landscape management: whose knowledge—scientific, local, Indigenous—has weight in decision-making?

Reference: Ch. 02 (Adaptive Epistemologies)

Epistemology

The philosophical study of knowledge and what counts as knowledge, how knowledge is produced, what makes a claim valid or justified. In this dissertation: an adaptive epistemology positions knowledge as provisional, situated, and continuously updated through practice.

Reference: Ch. 02 (Adaptive Epistemologies)

—

F

Feedback Loop / Feedback System

A system where output is fed back as input, creating a cycle of monitoring and response. Negative feedback (thermostatic) stabilizes systems; positive feedback (runaway) destabilizes them. Landscapes contain multiple overlapping feedback loops among hydrology, biology, human management, and computation.

Reference: Ch. 01, Ch. 09 (Interactions), Ch. 11, Ch. 02

—

G

Geomorphology / Geomorphological Model

The study of landforms and the processes that shape them (erosion, deposition, weathering). A geomorphological model simulates how sediment moves, deposits, and reshapes terrain under different flow conditions. Used in this dissertation to understand how river systems behave and adapt.

Reference: Ch. 08 (Landscape as Medium)

—

H

Hydrologic / Hydrology

The study of water movement through landscapes: precipitation, flow, infiltration, evaporation. Hydrologic processes shape and are shaped by landscape form, vegetation, and human infrastructure. Critical to understanding fluvial (river) systems and wetland management.

Reference: Ch. 08 (Landscape as Medium)

—

I

Indeterminacy

Quality of not being fully determined or predictable in advance. A landscape has indeterminacy when its future behavior cannot be completely specified from initial conditions. This is a feature, not a failure—indeterminacy allows for novelty and adaptation.

Reference: Ch. 02, Ch. 12

Interface

A boundary or contact surface where different systems meet and interact. In this dissertation: design interfaces (visualizations, controls, displays) through which humans perceive and act on landscapes; also the physical interfaces where technology meets ecology—a sensor array in wetland, a robotic system in a floodplain.

Reference: Ch. 05 (Tools), throughout

—

J

Justice / Environmental Justice / Social Justice

In landscape practice: the question of who benefits and who bears the burden from environmental management. Which communities have voice in decisions? Whose land is protected and whose is sacrificed? Justice is structurally embedded, not an afterthought.

Reference: Ch. 01 (Territory), Ch. 05, Ch. 07 (Technogeographies), Ch. 02, Ch. 11

—

L

Landscape Architecture

The design of land at various scales, from gardens to regions, addressing aesthetic, ecological, social, and functional dimensions. This dissertation argues that landscape architecture is a form of knowledge production, not solely form making or beautification, and that its epistemological stakes are political and ethical.

Reference: Throughout

—

M

Media Theory

The study of how communication technologies shape what can be known and how we think. In this dissertation: maps, models, sensor networks, and visualizations are media—they don't simply represent reality, they shape perception and possibility.

Reference: Ch. 05 (Tools), Ch. 07 (Technogeographies)

Model / Modeling

A representation of a system (physical, computational, or hybrid) that captures selected aspects of behavior for study or speculation. This dissertation argues models are hypotheses, not predictions—they reveal what we're measuring and what we're ignoring.

Reference: Ch. 06 (Models), Ch. 05, Ch. 09 (Interactions), throughout

—

N

Neo-Wilds

A conceptual category distinct from "wilderness" (pristine, untouched) or "tame landscape" (fully managed). Neo-wilds are territories shaped by both human intention and autonomous ecological processes, where design and nature continuously negotiate. Synthetic, hybrid, not returning to an imagined pre-human state.

Reference: Ch. 07 (Technogeographies), Ch. 02, Ch. 11

—

O

Optimization

The process of tuning a system to maximize or minimize a particular variable (efficiency, yield, profit). This dissertation critiques over-reliance on optimization: optimizing for one output often compromises resilience, diversity, or social equity.

Reference: Ch. 01, Ch. 06, Ch. 10

—

P

Political Ecology

A field of study examining how power structures, institutions, and interests shape environmental management and ecological change. Not all environmental problems have purely technical solutions—they involve competing values and unequal power. Applied to landscape: studying who gets to decide which landscapes get protected, restored, or sacrificed.

Reference: Ch. 01 (Territory), Ch. 05, Ch. 07

Practice-Based Research / Research Through Design

A methodology treating creative/design practice itself as research, not merely as illustration of prior theory. Projects, prototypes, and experiments generate knowledge. The practice is the primary research move, not a secondary artifact.

Reference: Ch. 03 (Refractions), throughout

Proposition / Speculative Design

An untested idea expressed in tangible form—a drawing, model, or prototype—deployed to ask "what if?" rather than to predict "what will be." Design propositions are hypotheses about how landscapes might behave or be organized under new conditions.

Reference: Ch. 05 (Tools), Ch. 06, throughout

—

R

Reflexive / Reflection

Turning attention back on one's own practice, assumptions, and effects. Reflexive stewardship means continuously questioning your own role in landscape management: What am I assuming? Who benefits? What is being excluded? This prevents stagnation and opens possibility for adaptation.

Reference: Ch. 03 (Refractions), Ch. 02, Ch. 11

Reflexive Stewardship

Care for landscapes based on ongoing reflection about one's own agency, limitations, and complicity. Not domination, not abandonment, but engaged responsibility with humility. Stewardship as a continuous practice of learning and adjustment.

Reference: Ch. 01, Ch. 02, Ch. 11

Regime Shift / Tipping Point

A threshold in a system beyond which behavior changes qualitatively and irreversibly (or with great difficulty). Ecosystems can cross tipping points abruptly with little warning. Adaptive management must account for the possibility of sudden shifts, not assume gradual change.

Reference: Ch. 01, Ch. 02

Resilience / Resilient System

Capacity of a system to withstand disturbance and recover to its prior state. Distinguished from antifragility: a resilient system bounces back where an antifragile system is strengthened by disturbance. Landscape design should aim for antifragility, not just resilience.

Reference: Ch. 01, Ch. 09, Ch. 02

—

S

Science and Technology Studies (STS)

An interdisciplinary field examining how scientific knowledge and technological systems are produced, contested, and embedded in social and political structures. Not treating science as neutral truth-discovery but as a social and material practice. Applied here: how does the choice of sensing apparatus shape environmental knowledge?

Reference: Ch. 05 (Tools), Ch. 06, Ch. 07

Sensing / Sensor Network / Environmental Monitoring

Technologies that measure environmental variables (temperature, moisture, contaminants, species presence, etc.). Sensors actively constitute what becomes "data," which territories get monitored, and which environmental crises become visible to decision-makers.

Reference: Ch. 05 (Tools), Ch. 07 (Technogeographies)

Solutionism

Evgeny Morozov's critique: the assumption that every problem has a technological solution, if only we're clever enough. Solutionism mistakes problem-solving for problem-prevention and assumes technology can bypass political conflict. This dissertation argues against solutionism: some problems require negotiation, not innovation.

Reference: Ch. 03 (Refractions), Ch. 02

Speculative Realism / Object-Oriented Ontology (OOO)

Philosophical movements questioning the human-centered view of reality. Objects (rivers, algorithms, machines) have their own being and agency, not merely as reflections of human meaning-making. Applied to landscape: sediment, plants, and computational systems are not just passive recipients of human design.

Reference: Ch. 11 (Co-Creation), Ch. 07

Synthetic Ground

Hybrid territories produced by the interweaving of biological, technical, and social systems. Not "synthetic" in the sense of artificial or fake, but synthesized—created through the mixing of distinct elements. A wetland with sensor networks, a river managed by computational spillways, a forest managed by human and ecological agents together.

Reference: Ch. 01, Ch. 08 (Landscape as Medium), throughout

—

T

Technogeography / Technogeographies of Sensing

How technology and geography co-constitute each other: how sensing networks reshape which territories are visible to governance, how infrastructure shapes which landscapes become legible, and how political power operates through technical systems. Not technology layered onto geography, but an integrated inquiry into tech-nature relationships.

Reference: Ch. 07 (Technogeographies), Ch. 02, Ch. 11

Terrain Vague

Ignasi de Solà-Morales's term for liminal, indeterminate landscapes that resist full categorization or control—vacant lots, abandoned sites, edge conditions. Not "failed" landscapes but those that remain partially opaque, unpredictable, heterogeneous. A resistance to complete management.

Reference: Ch. 02

—

U

Unpredictability / Uncertainty

The condition of not being able to predict future behavior with certainty, even with complete present information. In complex systems, unpredictability is fundamental (sensitive dependence on initial conditions). Adaptive epistemology accepts unpredictability as permanent, not as a temporary gap waiting to be filled with more data.

Reference: Ch. 02 (Adaptive Epistemologies), throughout

—

V

Viridic / Viridic Autonomy

Julian Raxworthy's concept of biological autonomy and maintenance as a design medium. The viridic is the green—the aliveness and self-organizing capacity of living systems. Design works with viridic processes (growth, decay, regeneration), not against them.

Reference: Ch. 05, Ch. 11 (Co-Creation), Ch. 02

—

W

Wetware

Hybrid systems blending biological and computational elements. A river with robotic monitoring is wetware. A forest with embedded sensors is wetware. The term emphasizes that the biology and computation are inseparably intertwined, not layers on top of each other.

Reference: Ch. 05, Ch. 10, Ch. 11, Ch. 02

Wildness / Wild / Wilderness

Conceptually distinguished from neo-wilds. Wildness traditionally means untouched by human hand (an increasingly impossible category in the Anthropocene). This dissertation argues for neo-wilds—territories shaped by both human intention and autonomous ecological processes—without pretense of returning to pre-human conditions.

Reference: Ch. 07, Ch. 11
